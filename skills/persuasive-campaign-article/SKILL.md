# Persuasive Campaign Article

Write persuasion that survives scrutiny. The job is not to sound persuasive; it is to move the right reader toward a justified action using a strong argument, credible evidence, clear stakes, and low-friction next steps.

## Operating rules

- Identify the exact campaign action before writing.
- Research current claims that materially affect credibility. Prefer primary and authoritative sources.
- Never invent statistics, testimonials, endorsements, urgency, scarcity, quotes, outcomes, partners, or social proof.
- Separate facts, interpretation, projections, and campaign claims.
- Do not use emotional intensity to disguise weak evidence.
- Avoid generic AI-copy patterns: “In today’s rapidly changing world,” empty superlatives, repetitive rhetorical questions, fake intimacy, inflated urgency, and CTA spam.
- Preserve the audience's dignity and agency. The reader should understand what they are joining and why.
- For political, civic, public-policy, health, finance, legal, or other high-stakes campaigns, increase source rigor and avoid unsupported causal claims.
- If the campaign involves testimonials, reviews, endorsements, incentives, or creator promotion, flag relevant disclosure/compliance requirements rather than fabricating proof.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the conversion event

Capture:
- campaign goal;
- exact CTA;
- target audience;
- desired reader state before and after reading;
- offer/commitment requested;
- source of traffic/distribution channel;
- campaign deadline or timing, if real;
- objections, risks, and trust barriers;
- available proof and evidence.

Write one sentence:

`We want [specific audience] to take [specific action] because [credible reason] despite [main objection/friction].`

### 2. Research the persuasion environment

For time-sensitive or factual campaigns, research before drafting:
- current facts and statistics;
- audience language and concerns;
- competing narratives and counterarguments;
- existing alternatives;
- credible experts, documents, cases, first-party evidence, or public records;
- current search and media framing when discoverability matters.

Build an evidence table with:
- claim;
- evidence/source;
- confidence;
- what the evidence actually proves;
- what it does *not* prove.

Do not draft a strong claim until its evidentiary burden is understood.

### 3. Build the argument map

Use this sequence where appropriate:
1. **Reader reality** — show that you understand the situation they already recognize.
2. **Problem/stakes** — establish what is happening and why it matters.
3. **Mechanism** — explain why the problem exists, not just that it exists.
4. **Campaign thesis** — state what should change.
5. **Evidence** — substantiate the thesis with concrete proof.
6. **Counterargument** — present the strongest reasonable objection fairly.
7. **Response** — answer it with evidence or acknowledge limits.
8. **Agency** — explain what the reader's action contributes.
9. **CTA** — make the next action specific, credible, and proportionate.

Do not force every campaign into a melodramatic hero-villain structure.

### 4. Choose the persuasion mode

Select the dominant mode based on the campaign:
- **Evidence-led** for skeptical, professional, technical, policy, B2B, or institutional audiences.
- **Story-led** when lived experience makes the stakes understandable.
- **Identity/community-led** when belonging and shared standards genuinely matter.
- **Urgency-led** only when urgency is real and verifiable.
- **Opportunity-led** when participation creates clear upside rather than preventing loss.

Blend modes deliberately; do not pile every persuasion technique into one article.

### 5. Design the information architecture

Default structure:
- headline;
- subheadline/deck;
- opening hook;
- stakes/problem;
- mechanism/explanation;
- proof blocks;
- objection handling;
- campaign solution or proposition;
- what participation means;
- primary CTA;
- optional FAQ or proof appendix.

For mobile reading:
- keep paragraphs short;
- use descriptive subheads;
- front-load meaning;
- avoid bullet lists unless they genuinely improve scanning;
- make proof easy to verify.

### 6. Write headlines with substance

Generate multiple headline routes, then choose by audience and channel:
- concrete consequence;
- surprising but defensible fact;
- sharp tension;
- specific opportunity;
- direct invitation;
- contrarian thesis supported by evidence.

Reject clickbait whose body cannot repay the promise.

### 7. Write the opening

The opening should accomplish at least two of these quickly:
- establish relevance;
- create a knowledge gap;
- show stakes;
- present an unexpected fact;
- introduce a credible human situation;
- surface the central tension.

Avoid generic scene-setting.

### 8. Build proof architecture

Use a mix of appropriate proof forms:
- first-party data;
- independent research;
- public records;
- expert analysis;
- case evidence;
- demonstrations;
- direct observations;
- verified testimonials or participant stories;
- transparent calculations.

For every important number, ask: “Compared with what?” Add denominator, baseline, timeframe, and scope when necessary.

### 9. Handle objections explicitly

List the strongest reasons a reasonable reader might not act:
- disbelief;
- distrust;
- cost;
- time;
- inconvenience;
- reputational risk;
- uncertainty about impact;
- disagreement with the proposed solution;
- fear of spam or future obligations.

Answer only objections the campaign can answer honestly. When a limitation is real, disclose it.

### 10. Design the CTA system

Specify:
- primary action;
- what happens after clicking;
- time/effort required;
- what the participant receives or changes;
- any genuine deadline;
- privacy or commitment implications where relevant.

Use one dominant CTA. Secondary CTAs should support readers not yet ready for the primary action, e.g. read evidence, subscribe, share, attend an information session.

### 11. Optimize for 2026 discovery and answer surfaces

When the article will live publicly on the web:
- answer the central question clearly near the top;
- use descriptive headings that map to real audience questions;
- define entities, campaign purpose, dates, places, organizations, and actions unambiguously;
- make claims attributable and sources easy to inspect;
- include original evidence, analysis, examples, or primary information that gives search engines and AI answer systems a reason to cite the page;
- avoid keyword stuffing and formulaic “SEO copy”;
- create concise passages that can stand alone without stripping away necessary context;
- update materially changed facts rather than adding cosmetic “updated” labels.

Discoverability is downstream of usefulness and source quality, not a replacement for them.

### 12. Run conversion QA

Before finalizing, score 1–5:
- audience relevance;
- clarity of problem;
- credibility;
- evidence quality;
- argument coherence;
- emotional resonance without manipulation;
- objection coverage;
- CTA clarity;
- friction reduction;
- originality/specificity;
- mobile readability;
- discoverability/answerability, if public web content.

Anything below 4 needs revision or an explicit limitation note.

### 13. Post-publish learning

When performance data exists, evaluate the funnel rather than blaming the copy immediately:
- impression/search/social reach;
- headline click-through;
- engaged reading/scroll depth;
- CTA exposure;
- CTA click rate;
- form/start rate;
- completion/conversion rate;
- downstream quality of participants/leads;
- source/channel differences.

Diagnose where the drop occurs. A weak offer, wrong audience, slow page, confusing form, low trust, or poor distribution can make excellent copy fail.

## Default deliverable

Unless the user requests only the article, return:
1. campaign objective and conversion event;
2. audience + objection map;
3. research/evidence summary;
4. core argument and message hierarchy;
5. 5–10 headline routes;
6. finished article;
7. CTA variants appropriate to the channel;
8. fact-check / unsupported-claim flags;
9. distribution and measurement plan;
10. next A/B test or learning hypothesis.

If the user asks only for a finished article, still do the research and reasoning internally, then deliver the final copy without burdening them with the full framework unless useful.
